# Chapter 9: Preference Data and Reward Models

Chapters 2-8 evaluated behaviour after a model already existed. This chapter moves one step earlier and builds the machinery used to shape that behaviour in the first place: a reward model trained on pairwise human preferences. A reward model is treated exactly the way Chapters 3-4 treated a safety classifier, understand the simplest version completely (a linear model on frozen representations, which turns out to be logistic regression on differenced features), then add a small amount of complexity, using the same Qwen/Qwen3-0.6B model as Chapters 6-8, now as a frozen embedding extractor rather than a generator.

The notebook [`chapter_9_preference_data_and_reward_models.ipynb`](chapter_9_preference_data_and_reward_models.ipynb) reproduces the chapter's experiments end to end. Running it top to bottom will:

- load PKU-SafeRLHF (roughly 82,000 preference pairs) and audit it before training anything: the helpfulness/safety agreement rate, a full safety-combination breakdown, severity and response-source distributions, exact-duplicate and repeated-prompt checks, and a prompt-disjoint fit/validation split built with `GroupShuffleSplit`;
- derive the Bradley-Terry preference probability and pairwise loss from first principles, with a worked numeric sanity check, and show explicitly why a linear reward model reduces to logistic regression on the difference between two response embeddings;
- encode prompt-response pairs with Qwen3-0.6B (mean-pooled hidden states) and fit helpfulness and safety reward directions two ways, `scikit-learn` logistic regression on difference features, and an equivalent PyTorch scalar head trained with the actual Bradley-Terry loss, confirming the two agree;
- train a small MLP reward head as a comparison and show its overfitting signature directly (falling training loss alongside rising validation loss);
- audit both reward heads properly: margin distributions and the most confidently wrong pairs, calibration (reusing Chapter 4's reliability-diagram machinery), bootstrap confidence intervals, accuracy broken down by safety-pair combination and severity level, and accuracy specifically on the rows where helpfulness and safety disagree;
- treat helpfulness and safety as a genuine multi-objective problem: standardize both heads' scores before combining them, and compute an actual Pareto frontier rather than picking one scalarization weight; and
- evaluate both reward heads on RewardBench 2 as an external generalization check, broken down by its Factuality / Focus / Math / Precise IF / Safety subsets.

## What the results actually looked like

Run with this notebook's default (small) sample size on Qwen3-0.6B, the audit surfaced almost every pattern the chapter predicts, not a smoothed-over illustration:

- **Agreement was lowest exactly where the book predicts**: when both responses share the same safety label (`both_safe`: 74.0% agreement, `both_unsafe`: 75.3%), agreement between helpfulness and safety preferences was lower than when one response was clearly unsafe and the other clearly safe (85.7%). The easy case really is easy; the hard case really is hard.
- **The safety reward head was worst exactly on the "both unsafe" pairs** (accuracy 0.507, indistinguishable from chance) and **on the highest-severity pairs** (severity level 3: accuracy 0.421, *below* chance), and **exactly at chance on the rows where helpfulness and safety disagree** (0.500). These are the precise failure modes the chapter's audit design was built to surface, not generic noise.
- **The MLP head's overfitting was visible directly in the numbers**: training loss fell from 0.67 to 0.05 over 20 epochs while validation loss *rose* from 0.66 to 1.04, even though its final point-accuracy ticked up slightly over the linear head. A single accuracy comparison would have missed this entirely.
- **RewardBench 2 transfer matched the chapter's own prediction**: the safety-trained head scored highest specifically on RewardBench's Safety subset (0.75) while scoring no better than chance on Math, exactly the "narrower preference concept, not a general-purpose reward" pattern the chapter warns to expect.

## Data and dependencies

PKU-SafeRLHF and RewardBench 2 are both downloaded automatically via the `datasets` library (no redistribution here); PKU-SafeRLHF is released under CC BY-NC 4.0. Cached response embeddings are written to `data/processed/reward_embeddings/` and are gitignored, they are regenerated by running the notebook. No new dependencies beyond what earlier chapters already require (`torch`, `transformers`, `datasets`, `scikit-learn`, `scipy`).

## Running it

From the repository root:

```bash
pip install -r requirements.txt
jupyter notebook "Chapter 9/chapter_9_preference_data_and_reward_models.ipynb"
```

The expensive step is encoding text with Qwen3-0.6B (roughly 0.4 seconds per response on Apple Silicon MPS), not training the reward heads themselves, once embeddings are cached, fitting and evaluating both heads takes seconds. The default `N_FIT = 500`, `N_VAL = 150` keep total runtime to a few minutes; raise them toward the book's suggested 5,000-10,000 pairs, encoding time scales roughly linearly, to reproduce the full-scale experiment with real statistical power behind the subgroup and bootstrap results.
